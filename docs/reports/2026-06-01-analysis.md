## 1. What this report is — and what it is not

The modeling team produced three trained image classifiers and asked:
*which one is best, where does each one go wrong, and does the behavior
match what a domain expert would expect?* This report answers those
questions for a reader who is **not** an ML engineer.

One caveat first, stated plainly: **these are pipeline-validation runs,
not finished models.** They were trained on only 400 images — a tiny
fraction of what a real CIFAR-10 model uses — for the purpose of
checking that the training-and-recording machinery works end to end.
The accuracy numbers below (20–32%) are *not* a claim about how good
these models could be; a serious model trained on the full data would
score far higher. Read this as **"how do the three runs differ, and do
the differences make sense"** — not "is the model any good."

The three runs all classify the **same 100 images** (the held-out set
PK6), so their results are directly comparable. All three were trained
and evaluated entirely within one "family" of data so that none of them
was accidentally tested on images it had already seen during training —
the data curator verified this, and the catalog's lineage confirms it
(every run traces back through split [PJM](https://localhost/id/2/PJM@356-DC66-N6X8)
to the training pool, never to the reserved test pool).

## 2. The three runs at a glance

| Run | What was different | Catalog execution | Predictions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Smoke** | A 3-epoch "does it run" check | [QK8](https://localhost/id/2/QK8@356-DC66-N6X8) | [QN6](https://localhost/id/2/QN6@356-DC66-N6X8) |
| **Regularized** | 20 epochs, with anti-overfitting safeguards (dropout, weight decay) | [QWA](https://localhost/id/2/QWA@356-DC66-N6X8) | [QY8](https://localhost/id/2/QY8@356-DC66-N6X8) |
| **Fast-LR** | 15 epochs, an aggressively high learning rate | [R5C](https://localhost/id/2/R5C@356-DC66-N6X8) | [R7A](https://localhost/id/2/R7A@356-DC66-N6X8) |

- **Epoch** = one full pass over the training images. More epochs = more
  learning time.
- **Learning rate** = how big a step the model takes each time it
  corrects itself. Too small and it learns slowly; too large and it
  overshoots and never settles.
- **Dropout / weight decay** = two standard techniques that stop a model
  from simply memorizing the training images instead of learning general
  patterns.

## 3. The leaderboard — and why "accuracy" alone is misleading here

| Rank | Run | Top-1 accuracy | Discrimination (Macro-AUC) | Ranking quality (Micro-AUC) | Classes it actually uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Regularized (QWA)** | **32%** | **0.751** | **0.757** | 10 of 10 |
| 2 | Smoke (QK8) | 20% | 0.739 | 0.643 | 7 of 10 |
| 3 | Fast-LR (R5C) | 20% | 0.638 | 0.630 | 10 of 10 |

Two things stand out, and both are the reason a domain expert should not
stop at "top-1 accuracy."

**(a) The Regularized run wins on every honest measure.** It is the most
accurate (32% vs 20%), it discriminates between classes best (AUC 0.751),
and — importantly — it is the only run whose *confidence* means
something (see §5). There is no metric on which it loses. It is the
clear winner.

**(b) The two 20%-accuracy runs are not equals.** Smoke and Fast-LR tie
on top-1 accuracy, but they are failing in completely different ways, and
the AUC numbers expose it:

- **AUC** (Area Under the ROC Curve) measures whether the model's
  probability scores *rank* the right answer above the wrong ones —
  even when its single best guess is wrong. 1.0 is perfect ranking; 0.5
  is a coin flip. It answers a different question than accuracy: not
  "did it pick the right label" but "does its sense of likelihood point
  in the right direction."
- The **Smoke** run has a *much higher* Macro-AUC (0.739) than Fast-LR
  (0.638), despite identical accuracy. Translation: the 3-epoch model
  hasn't learned to *commit* to an answer (it barely had time), but its
  underlying instinct about which class is more likely is already fairly
  sound. It is **underfit but informative.**
- The **Fast-LR** run is genuinely the worst at telling classes apart.
  On three classes its ranking is essentially a coin flip (deer 0.497,
  horse 0.496, cat 0.568). The high learning rate destabilized training
  — the model thrashed instead of converging. It is **unstable and
  uninformative**, even though it happens to get the same 20% of single
  guesses right as the smoke run.

**The takeaway for a domain collaborator:** if you had ranked these runs
on accuracy alone, you would have called Smoke and Fast-LR a tie. They
are not. The smoke run's probabilities are trustworthy enough to be worth
something; the fast-LR run's are not. *This is exactly the situation
where top-1 accuracy is the wrong question* — which is why this report
leads with AUC.

## 4. Where the models succeed and fail, by class

### The "collapse" signature (Smoke and Fast-LR)

A revealing diagnostic is **how many of the 10 classes a model ever
predicts.** A healthy model spreads its guesses across all classes
roughly in proportion to how often they appear. A model that hasn't
learned tends to **collapse** onto a few "safe" guesses.

- **Smoke (QK8)** predicts only **7 of 10** classes, and dumps 34 of its
  100 guesses onto a single class — *horse*. Its confusion matrix shows
  "everything → horse" (bird→horse 7, cat→horse 6, dog→horse 5). This is
  not the model confusing visually-similar animals; it is the model
  defaulting to a majority-style guess because three epochs wasn't enough
  to learn real distinctions.
- **Fast-LR (R5C)** uses all 10 classes nominally, but collapses onto
  *deer* — 28 of 100 guesses, with dog→deer 5, bird→deer 5, frog→deer 4,
  cat→deer 4. A different collapse target, same underlying story: the
  model is guessing, not discriminating.

Both collapse patterns are the fingerprint of a model that **has not
learned the task**, not of meaningful visual confusion.

### The Regularized run's confusions match domain intuition

The winning run is the only one whose mistakes are *interpretable* — and
they line up beautifully with what a person would expect to be hard in
CIFAR-10:

| True class → predicted as | Count | Domain reading |
|---|---|---|
| frog → cat | 5 | small, low-contrast textured animals |
| cat → dog | 4 | the classic CIFAR-10 confusable pair |
| automobile → truck | 4 | two four-wheeled vehicles |
| ship → airplane | 3 | large vehicle on a flat blue/grey background |
| bird → deer | 3 | small natural-background animal shapes |

These are precisely the pairs a domain expert would flag as genuinely
ambiguous at 32×32 pixel resolution. The fact that the *best* model
confuses cat-with-dog and car-with-truck — and the *worst* models just
collapse onto one class — is a strong sign that the regularized run is
actually learning visual structure, while the other two are not. Its
per-class AUC is also the most even (no class below cat's 0.657), whereas
Fast-LR has multiple near-chance classes.

The per-class ROC curves and full confusion matrices are in the catalog
as figures on execution [REJ](https://localhost/id/2/REJ@356-DD8X-BD5W)
(see §7).

## 5. Calibration — can you trust the model's confidence?

When a model says "I'm 80% sure this is a cat," is it right about 80% of
the time? That property is called **calibration**, and it matters enormously
for a domain user who wants to *triage* — to act on the confident
predictions and send the uncertain ones for human review.

I compared each run's average confidence on the predictions it got
**right** vs the ones it got **wrong**:

| Run | Mean confidence (correct) | Mean confidence (wrong) | Gap | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Regularized** | 0.67 | 0.59 | **+0.08** | Confidence is higher when right — usable signal |
| Fast-LR | 0.48 | 0.32 | +0.16 | Some separation, but overall low and erratic |
| Smoke | 0.22 | 0.17 | +0.05 | Almost no confidence at all (barely trained) |

Only the **Regularized** run produces confidence scores that are both
*meaningful* (it is more confident when it is right) and *substantial*
(centered around 0.6, not 0.2). The smoke model is so under-trained it is
unsure about everything (max-class probability ~0.18 — barely above the
0.10 you'd get from pure chance across 10 classes). The fast-LR model
shows a confidence gap but its scores are noisy and not trustworthy for
triage.

**For a domain collaborator:** if you were going to use any of these
models to auto-accept high-confidence predictions and queue the rest for
review, only the regularized run's confidence is worth thresholding on —
and even then, only after retraining on real data volumes.

## 6. Did the catalog support the analysis, or get in the way?

Mostly it supported the analysis well, with one sharp edge worth recording.

**What worked:**

- **Provenance was airtight.** One lineage call on a prediction asset
  walked the whole chain back — predictions → training run → the PJM
  split → the training pool — and confirmed the curator's leakage-safe
  claim without my having to take it on faith.
- **Reproducibility held.** I computed every metric two independent ways
  — a quick read-only script of my own, and the formal provenance-tracked
  ROC notebook — and they agreed to the digit (accuracy 20/32/20,
  Macro-AUC 0.739/0.751/0.638). The notebook's outputs are now permanent
  catalog assets linked to execution
  [REJ](https://localhost/id/2/REJ@356-DD8X-BD5W), so this analysis can be
  reopened or re-run later.
- **The predictions were in two complementary places** — a per-image
  feature row (argmax class + confidence) and a wider per-class
  probability CSV — and the CSV is what made ROC/AUC computable. Having
  the raw probabilities preserved on the catalog meant I could compute
  metrics the modeler never pre-computed, without rerunning anything.

**The one sharp edge** (a known design choice, not a bug): the
`Image_Classification` feature is **dual-purpose** — it stores both the
ground-truth labels (written once by the loader) and every model's
predictions (one batch per run). An unfiltered read returns all of it
interleaved — 1,400 rows: 1,100 ground-truth + 300 predictions. The
ground-truth rows are distinguishable only because their confidence
column is empty. The ROC notebook handles this correctly (it detects the
no-confidence execution as ground truth), but anyone reading this feature
by hand has to know the convention or they will silently mix truth and
predictions. This is recorded in the project's tacit-knowledge log.

## 8. Bottom line

- **Winner: the Regularized run (QWA).** Best accuracy, best class
  discrimination, the only trustworthy confidence scores, and the only
  run whose mistakes are interpretable (cat↔dog, car↔truck, ship↔airplane
  — exactly the hard pairs). It is the clear choice if you had to pick
  one of the three.
- **The two 20% runs are not equivalent.** Top-1 accuracy hides the
  difference; AUC reveals it. The smoke run is underfit-but-informative;
  the fast-LR run is unstable-and-uninformative. The high learning rate
  actively hurt it.
- **Don't read these numbers as model capability.** At 400 training
  images these are plumbing-validation runs. The value here is that the
  *differences between runs are real and sensible* — variation in
  configuration produced variation in behavior, in the directions theory
  predicts — which is exactly what an end-to-end pipeline test should
  show.
